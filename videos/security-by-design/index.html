Transcript

01 · 0:00

PRP is a communication protocol that connects applications through cryptographic identities. They establish relationships and control access permissions. Communication can be direct or pass through aggregators: network participants that bring together and forward communications for other participants. That role does not give them access to content protected between the endpoints. How does this architecture protect against impersonation, monitoring, and attacks?

02 · 0:33

Copying a name or address is not enough to impersonate another identity. The peer proves control of its key. A person's or company's identity can be verified separately.

03 · 0:45

On protected channels, the provider carries encrypted data. It does not receive the conversation's keys. Authentication and sequence checks reject tampering and unauthorized replay.

04 · 0:58

Different relationships can use different identities. An aggregator knows its local peers and forwarding context. It does not need to know the protected conversation's original source or final destination. A rendezvous is an intermediary where the paths built by the two endpoints meet. Each endpoint can know its own path to that meeting point without receiving the other side's complete path. Joining the two sides does not require revealing the complete end-to-end trajectory to any participant. With opaque packets of constant size sent at fixed intervals, real activity no longer changes the observable traffic pattern. Cover traffic maintains the same pattern with or without real data, removing those variations as clues for correlation.

05 · 1:48

A path can be blocked. When reachable alternatives exist, participants can choose other intermediaries, including networks in other countries. Carrying a segment does not require knowing the protected final identity. This resistance depends on available paths.

06 · 2:07

DDoS protection has two fronts. Admission challenges, proof of work, and state limits constrain the processing and memory spent on requests. To protect subsequent links, each participant enforces forwarding authorization before retransmitting. Rejected traffic need not travel across the network to the victim.

07 · 2:31

Being recognized does not grant unrestricted access. Permissions belong to the relationship and service. Access to one resource does not open the others, and changing paths does not silently expand that authorization.

08 · 2:46

The result is less trust placed in the path, verifiable peer identity, and explicit limits on traffic. PRP.
